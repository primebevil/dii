# ADR-0016: Consumer Admission and Identity

Status: Accepted as a direction, 2026-07-27, from the Week-4 close-out following ADR-0015. Builds on ADR-0002, ADR-0004, ADR-0009, ADR-0013, ADR-0014, and ADR-0015. Rests on docs/Governance_And_Abuse_Resistance.md and docs/Identity_Note_From_Prototype.md. This record commits a shape and a direction, not a final mechanism. It has no prototype behind it the way ADR-0011 did, and legal review is deferred, so the credential mechanism, the denylist governance, and a legal pass are named below as gates that must close before anything ships.

## Context

ADR-0015 made access distribution the thesis and promoted "who can use them" from a loose end to the next load-bearing decision. The Week-3 prototype stubbed identity as one shared consumer token, and the identity note that came out of the build recorded what the ingress actually had to verify. The governance notes then worked through the abuse cases, banning a consumer, isolating a bad pod, fair-use, data handling, and moderation, and named a preferred answer to the central fork. This record turns that reasoning into a decision.

It also narrows the problem. The identity note surfaced three needs: node admission, per-consumer credentials, and caller attribution across hops. Two of those, node admission and cross-hop attribution, exist to make node-to-node overflow safe, and ADR-0015 made overflow an optional optimization rather than the core. So the load-bearing question is no longer the node-to-node handshake. It is how a stranger becomes an admitted consumer at a serving pod, how that access is revoked, and how a ban travels between pods without rebuilding a chokepoint. Node and member identity are out of scope here and deferred to an overflow-optional follow-on.

## Decision

Admission and revocation live at the pod, never at a center, extending ADR-0013. The two promises stay held apart: no single actor can switch off the network, and no one has a right to be served by a given pod. A pod may always refuse, blocking is cheap and local, and a bad actor losing access pod by pod is decentralized moderation working rather than the chokepoint the project forbids.

Consumers are admitted through three paths under one identity spine. A sponsored consumer needs only a base URL and a token, the two fields every OpenAI-compatible client already has, so the prototype's stub token remains valid for this case and no bespoke client is required. An affiliated consumer is admitted by delegation: the pod trusts an external identity source that already proofs identity, such as a library's patron accounts or an employer's single sign-on, the operator approves one policy rather than each person, and the pod auto-issues a scoped, session-bound token while keeping no user database of its own. The external anchor supplies the Sybil resistance, inherited rather than reinvented. For the unaffiliated stranger with no institution to borrow trust from, the committed direction is the middle path, between a fully decentralized free-for-all where a banned actor simply pod-hops, and a steward-run passport where the steward becomes a PII controller and a soft chokepoint. In the middle path, pods delegate to external identity anchors so DII itself holds no personal data, and the steward publishes opt-in, multi-publisher denylist feeds on the Fediverse defederation model, so bans propagate without a central account registry.

Revocation rests on a spine that cannot be avoided: cross-pod revocation is a stable cross-pod pseudonym plus a shared denylist. The stable pseudonym is computed transiently at admission only to run the denylist check, and persisted only for actual bad actors, so ordinary users authenticate with per-pod, unlinkable pseudonyms and leave no cross-pod footprint, while only the guilty acquire a durable cross-pod identity. Real personal data lives only at the external anchor; an optional identity broker that turns a login into an opaque handle is stateless and off the data path; a pod holds only the mapping from token to opaque handle to fair-use counters, ephemeral, and never joins the content stream, what was asked, to the identity stream, who asked. The bright line is firm: a small, negative, severe-only denylist is acceptable, and a per-person behavioral reputation record is the surveillance honeypot the project refuses outright.

This record commits that shape. It does not choose the concrete credential mechanism, does not settle how the denylist is governed, and does not clear the legal questions. Those are gates, listed below, that must close before any of this ships.

## Consequences

The design carries four residual risks, accepted here as real and unsolved rather than smoothed over, because naming them is the honest cost of committing the direction.

First, the model is strongest for the consumers who need DII least and weakest for those who need it most. Delegated admission works precisely because it borrows Sybil resistance from an institution, but the unaffiliated, constrained user, the cut-off, the phone-only, the person in a restricted place, is the mission's whole reason to exist and is exactly the one with no anchor to delegate to. That user falls back on acquiring a fresh anchor, which the design admits raises cost rather than being a wall. Delegated admission handles the easy case; the hardest and most mission-central user stays on the weakest leg, and the ADR says so rather than letting delegation imply the problem is handled.

Second, the shared denylist reintroduces at the reputation layer a soft version of the chokepoint the architecture removed at the data layer. "No single off-switch" is a guarantee about the data path, and it holds there. But if the steward publishes denylist feeds and most public-serving pods subscribe, as is likely, the steward's list becomes a de facto control point over who is served nearly everywhere, even though it never touches a prompt. Keeping the feed genuinely plural and multi-publisher is what stops the reputation layer from re-centralizing, and that is a governance commitment, not a technical guarantee.

Third, the claim that DII holds no personal data is true for ordinary users but not for the one durable shared store. A denylist of stable pseudonyms tied to real bad actors is itself regulated personal data, and the party that publishes it is a data controller with accuracy, appeal, and erasure obligations. The smallest store in the design carries the largest legal weight, and it is the part with the least mechanism behind it so far.

Fourth, identity makes serving strangers safe-ish but does not remove operator liability. A public-serving pod runs inference on a volunteer's hardware, and serving strangers means carrying liability for what they generate, with sexual content involving minors as the reportable hard line. A safety model and a classifier reduce the exposure, they do not erase it, so the real bottleneck is less how strangers are admitted than whether anyone will volunteer to be a public door at all, which is the exit-relay recruitment problem ADR-0009 already flagged as the hardest supply task. Access distribution, now the thesis, puts that problem at the center.

On the positive side, pods need no user database, the prototype's stub token stays correct for the sponsored and private cases, existing OpenAI-compatible clients work unchanged, and the model composes cleanly with the pod as accountability boundary (ADR-0013) and with preemptible best-effort consumer work bounded by per-identity fair-use (ADR-0014).

## Open questions

The concrete credential mechanism is unchosen. DIDs, DNSSEC, and pod-issued keys or broker-issued handles should be judged against the identity note's needs and validated by a delegated-admission prototype before anything ships, on the same principle that let the transport ADR follow the prototype rather than precede it.

Denylist governance is the hard human problem this record deliberately leaves open: who may list, how a listing is appealed, who corrects errors, and how a pod checks membership without downloading a browsable list of the banned. Its plurality is also what keeps the second consequence above from hardening into a chokepoint.

A legal review is mandatory before shipping, focused on the denylist as regulated personal data, on retention and logging defaults for public-serving pods, and on cross-border and subject-rights obligations. Shaping came first by intent; the legal pass cannot be skipped.

Node and member identity and cross-hop attribution are deferred as an overflow-optional follow-on, out of scope here by ADR-0015. Fair-use units and defaults, and how vouching resists abuse without becoming an exclusionary gate (inherited from ADR-0002), remain open as well.
